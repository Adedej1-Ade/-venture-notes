Mobisol: How Credit Risk Beat Good Capital

Victor Ade-Oduntan · August 2026

Mobisol failed in 2019, an early example of a nuance still relevant to climate businesses built around customer financing: how well a company manages its credit operations can matter as much to survival as the technology itself.

Mobisol was founded in 2010 by Thomas Gottschalk, out of Berlin, to bring electricity to rural East African households with no grid connection and no formal credit history. Its answer was the solar home system sold on hire purchase: a small down payment, then the balance paid over up to four years through mobile money, with a remote-lock mechanism disabling the system if payments lapsed. The system served as collateral, while the customer’s payment record substituted for a conventional credit file.

By the usual measures of growth, the model worked. By the time it filed for insolvency, Mobisol had installed more than 150,000 systems and reached roughly 750,000 people across Kenya, Tanzania, and Rwanda. It had raised around $90 million from backers including KfW, DEG, FMO, and IFC, and had disbursed around $150 million in consumer loans. Yet in April 2019, Mobisol filed for preliminary insolvency under German law. By September, Engie, the French utility, had acquired it out of the proceedings and folded it into Fenix International, its off-grid subsidiary.

Its traction points to neither a lack of capital nor a lack of customer demand, and the model itself was not unique. Zola Electric and d.light were using similar hire-purchase structures in the region, yet none collapsed in the same way. If the model itself was sound, the shortfall likely sat in how Mobisol ran it, and the clearest evidence of that sits in its loan book. Of the roughly $150 million it had lent to customers, the company recovered only around $25 million. In Tanzania, one of its three markets, the problem was visible on the ground well before the filing. Mobisol repossessed 5,700 solar home systems there in 2018, and by late that year, its Arusha office was recruiting a new Head of Credit. Taken together, that points to the quality of the credit being extended as one of the more important factors in Mobisol’s failure.

The broader lesson for climate ventures built around customer financing is that credit underwriting is a core operating capability, not a back-office function. Founders need to develop strong credit teams early, before growth outpaces the processes meant to manage it. As companies scale, a portfolio of performing customer receivables can itself become a source of institutional financing, making the quality of the underlying credit book important not only to cash flow and losses, but also to the company’s ability to raise the capital needed for further growth. Investors also need to diligence underwriting and collections as closely as technology, product, or founder quality. Mobisol had the capital, customer demand, and a structure that others had proven could work. The question is whether it had the credit operation needed to make that model work at scale.
