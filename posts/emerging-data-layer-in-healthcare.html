The Emerging Data Layer in Healthcare

Victor Ade-Oduntan · August 2026

As healthcare continues to digitise, a complementary layer is forming around aggregating and standardising data across fragmented systems. Globally, regulatory shifts and standards such as FHIR are making healthcare data more interoperable, enabling data infrastructure platforms that increasingly capture value as the access layer between providers and applications. This is already visible in the rise of companies such as Particle Health and Komodo Health, which sit horizontally across the ecosystem as data infrastructure and analytics layers.

In Europe, this trend is sharpest. The European Health Data Space requires member states to enable interoperable health data exchange by 2029. Country-level incumbents have built their systems on incompatible standards and have little incentive to make cross-border exchange easy, since doing so can weaken their own lock-in. Retrofitting those systems across 27 different national architectures is also technically complex and commercially unattractive. That creates an opening for a neutral, EHDS-native coordination layer built for cross-border exchange from the ground up, with regulation creating the conditions for a new entrant by making interoperability a requirement rather than an optional feature.

In African markets, the same underlying pressure exists. Healthcare is digitising and data volumes are growing, but the opportunity is shaped more by fragmentation than by a converging regulatory deadline. There is no continental equivalent to the EHDS forcing interoperability into existence. Africa CDC’s proposed Continental Health Data Governance Framework gained continental backing at the AU Summit in February 2026, with the Assembly encouraging Member States to accelerate its adoption. But unlike Europe’s EHDS, this does not yet amount to a binding interoperability regime: implementation remains dependent on national adoption and execution.

Even regulation would not resolve the problem on its own. There is no continent-wide identity layer that makes it easy to establish that records already held across different countries and health systems belong to the same person. Before data can be aggregated across the ecosystem, there is therefore the more basic challenge of matching and verifying patient records across fragmented systems and jurisdictions.

What this means is that Africa’s healthcare data layer is not yet being pulled together by regulation in the way Europe’s increasingly is. It remains early, fragmented and largely unresolved, with continental coordination still dependent on national adoption and no shared identity infrastructure underneath it. There are early signals of companies operating in this gap, such as Kera Health, but no dominant platform yet aggregating data across the ecosystem. Whether that gap eventually closes through continental regulation and shared identity infrastructure, or through a company solving the fragmentation commercially before either emerges, is one of the more interesting open questions in the space.
