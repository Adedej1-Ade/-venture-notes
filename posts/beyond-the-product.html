Beyond the Product: Defensibility in African B2B Fintech

Victor Ade-Oduntan · August 2026

African fintech is entering a new phase. The first generation was built largely on payments and wallets. The next wave is moving deeper into how businesses actually operate, with spend management, business banking, payroll, lending and other financial products increasingly embedded into business software itself.

Banks are already taking SME financial tooling more seriously, and competition at the payments layer is also intensifying. The growing penetration of PAPSS is one driver of this. As it scales cross-border interoperability across the continent, it could increase transaction volumes for PSPs by reducing friction on cross-border flows. At the same time, it standardises the underlying settlement rails that those flows depend on. As more providers connect to the same infrastructure, the rails themselves become less of a differentiator. For PSPs whose cross-border volume is meaningful, that could gradually sharpen competition and put pressure on monetisation models built purely on transactions.

This is pushing fintechs toward products such as spend management, where the customer relationship may end up being more valuable than the payment itself. Fintechs with large customer bases are also expanding into adjacent products. Moniepoint, for example, has moved beyond payments into business banking, expense management, accounting and working capital. Safaricom’s M-Pesa has followed a similar path in East Africa, growing from peer-to-peer transfers into business management tools, merchant payment systems and lending products such as Fuliza.

Moniepoint and M-Pesa are not outliers. They are early movers in what several industry reports describe as a sector-wide shift. A 2026 BCG report found that while payments currently generate 70 to 80 percent of African fintech revenue, new segments such as B2B financial services could make up half of industry revenue by 2030.

This creates a distinct problem for newly emerging companies in B2B fintech. If banks and larger fintechs can offer similar financial products, and are increasingly doing so, what actually makes a new company defensible?

One answer is vertical focus. A company serving pharmacies, for instance, can build around how pharmacies actually buy, sell and manage inventory. A logistics platform can build around fleet costs, receivables and supplier payments. The more closely a financial product is tied to a customer’s workflow, the harder it becomes to displace.

Distribution matters as well. Certain markets and business communities are difficult to reach through a broad, horizontal product. A company that earns trust within a specific group can often acquire customers more efficiently than a larger player entering later.

Geography can offer a similar advantage, though it is a less permanent one. Markets outside Nigeria’s largest fintech hubs may give smaller companies more time to build local relationships, understand regulation and establish distribution before larger players arrive.

The emerging companies most likely to hold up will therefore probably be doing at least two of these deliberately: building deep workflow relationships in a specific vertical, developing distribution that is difficult to replicate, or entering markets where larger players have not yet established dominance.
