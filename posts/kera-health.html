Kera Health: Addressing Africa’s Healthcare Data Gap

Victor Ade-Oduntan · August 2026

Africa’s healthcare systems are becoming increasingly digital, but the data they produce remains fragmented across hospitals, laboratories, pharmacies and insurers, making it hard to build a complete picture of a patient’s history or move that history between providers.

This is the problem Kera Health, a Senegalese venture, is trying to solve, by building an AI-powered platform that aggregates medical data and streamlines clinical and insurance workflows across Africa.

Founded in 2022 by co-founders Moustapha Cissé, Papa A. Sow and Hosam Mattar, the venture was backed by IFC with a $10 million Seed commitment in 2025, bringing disclosed funding to $13 million, and Kera is using it to expand beyond Senegal, into a broader African digital health market projected to grow from $3.8 billion in 2023 to $16.6 billion by 2030.

The real opportunity in making the data that Africa’s increasingly digitising healthcare processes produce work together is something no one on the continent has solved at the scale Kera is attempting. However, the gap in healthcare data interoperability all over the globe is already attracting attention: standards such as FHIR, developed by the US-based standards body HL7 but used globally, are making health data more interoperable, and companies such as Particle Health and Komodo Health have built businesses around connecting it. Regulation is also starting to move the same way: Europe’s Health Data Space has mandated member states to enable interoperable health data exchange by 2029, and even Africa CDC is developing a Continental Health Data Governance Framework, which pushes toward interoperable systems.

The harder question is bringing together the data that already exists for each person, spread across different systems and locations under different identifiers, and matching it to one identity. A name alone cannot do this. People share the same names, and records carry different spellings. Credit bureaus in Nigeria have faced a version of this problem, and BVN is the working solution, but the fix is still incomplete. Financial institutions in Nigeria increasingly tie credit information to BVN, but a name-based search can still surface history on the same person that a BVN-generated report does not capture.

Solving identity does not end the challenge, it strengthens an existing one. Once interoperability works, patient history can begin to shape how care is delivered, not just how it is recorded. Healthcare professionals will increasingly need to check a patient’s history before treating them, similar to how lenders analyse credit history before underwriting new credit. The problem is that this history matters most exactly when it is hardest to retrieve. An unconscious patient in an emergency room cannot provide identification at the moment their history may matter most. Interoperability makes that existing gap more consequential: the system may be capable of retrieving a patient’s history, but still unable to determine whose history it should retrieve.

For Kera Health, solving the health data gap means more than aggregating medical data. It means accurately aggregating that data under a reliable patient identifier, so that a provider can retrieve a patient’s health history and confidently link it to the person in front of them, no matter their condition.
