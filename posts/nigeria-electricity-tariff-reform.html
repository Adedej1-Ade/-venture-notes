Nigeria’s Electricity Tariff Reform: Diverging Implications for Solar and EVs

Victor Ade-Oduntan · September 2026

Nigeria’s electricity tariff reform is one of the more consequential shifts in the country’s energy space right now. The government is moving to strip away the subsidy that kept most bands cheap, with Band A already pushed closer to cost-reflective pricing since 2024 with pressure building to extend similar adjustments to other bands. The goal is to fund grid upgrades using the extra revenue, with the promise that higher prices now buy more reliable power later. Whether that promise holds decides which way two other trends move, solar and electric vehicles, and it pushes them in opposite directions.

Solar is already the main alternative Nigerians turn to once generator costs get too high, a shift that hardened in 2023 when the petrol subsidy was removed and generator fuel got more expensive. The tariff reform and the move to abolish the band system in states like Lagos adds to this, since customers would be paying more for grid power while reliability has not caught up. Band A customers are already complaining of paying premium rates without the promised twenty hours of daily supply, and DisCos have warned that Lagos State’s plan to abolish the band system could trigger blackouts if rushed. As long as this holds true, the case for solar, and for the financing models behind it, leasing, solar-as-a-service, blended finance, keeps getting stronger.

That window narrows if the reform actually delivers. If higher tariffs genuinely fund reliability improvements, the main reason people invest in backups like solar, to escape outages, weakens. While this would not affect the existing base of solar users who have fully purchased the assets, it could slow new adoption and put pressure on repayment rates for solar-as-a-service and leasing models. Current DisCo pushback and Band A misclassification issues suggest this outcome is not close, but it remains the structural risk underneath any solar thesis built on grid unreliability.

EVs sit on the opposite side of the same trade-off. Right now, grid instability hurts EV adoption rather than helping it, since charging needs power at a scale and reliability a shaky grid cannot deliver, unlike solar, which works at small household scale. This keeps EVs stuck in a chicken-and-egg problem, not enough cars to justify charging stations, not enough stations to justify buying a car. But if the tariff reform succeeds and the grid becomes reliable, that same outcome could be what finally unlocks EV charging infrastructure. For now, the government seems not to be betting on this. Its near-term fuel-substitution push is CNG, with hundreds of refuelling stations already rolling out, which leaves EVs as the more conditional, longer-dated side of this bet.
