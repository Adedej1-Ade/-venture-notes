Nigeria’s AI Investment Landscape: Understanding the Funding Gap and What Comes Next

Victor Ade-Oduntan · October 2026

Nigeria has recorded over 120 active AI startups spanning agritech, fintech, healthtech and language technology. However, only 16% of surveyed founders have raised actual venture capital; whether this reflects the proportion of companies considered investable, the fact that few investors have broadened their thesis to reflect the growing importance of AI in the Nigerian ecosystem, or both, is difficult to determine, though broader ecosystem investment trends offer compelling insight into the factors shaping this outcome.

Africa-wide, AI-related companies accounted for about 14% of funding in the first half of 2026, while AI-native companies building their own models raised less than 2%. Nigeria, as one of Africa’s largest AI markets, sits squarely within this unsurprising split. The attraction in Application AI is that it carries a fraction of the capital intensity that training a foundational model requires, and its defensibility does not depend on the model itself, which is increasingly commoditised, but on how deeply the company understands and embeds itself in one specific problem, giving it a data and workflow advantage that a generic model cannot easily replicate. AI-native companies, by contrast, face both the higher cost of building a model and the weaker moat of owning technology that is becoming easier to access off the shelf.

The funding pattern also suggests that AI investment in Nigeria is leaning B2B. About half of Africa’s AI-related funding has gone into fintech applications such as fraud detection, credit scoring and payments, all sold to other businesses, lenders, banks and insurers, rather than to individual consumers. This is not surprising, since African fintech is moving from payments and wallets into deeper business tooling such as spend management, business banking and lending, a shift compounded by PAPSS standardising settlement rails and pushing PSPs toward products beyond the payment itself. As that shift deepens, the problem fintechs increasingly need AI for is not moving money but deciding who deserves credit, using the transaction and business data these B2B relationships generate; this is why AI investment in Nigeria’s fintech sector is likely to stay concentrated in B2B, data-driven use cases.

This same pattern, application layer over model layer, is likely to hold across every sector AI touches in Nigeria in the near term, not just financial services. Beyond the capital intensity already working against AI-native bets, capital also tends to concentrate where it is already flowing, since a sector attracting visible funding and successful raises becomes easier for the next investor to underwrite, backed by the confidence that a pool of other investors is already thinking seriously about that same product category, which supports the success of new bets. For now, that pattern points squarely at fintech, and at application-layer AI more broadly, as the more fundable bet in Nigeria than any attempt to build AI from the ground up.
