Execution Is the New Moat: PAYGo Solar and Battery Swapping in Africa

Victor Ade-Oduntan · September 2026

Capital in certain climate tech niches is not spreading out as the market grows. It is concentrating into a shrinking set of already-scaled players, while newer entrants running similar playbooks struggle to attract the capital needed to catch up. Across African ClimateTech, the top 20 companies have captured 60% of all funding raised since 2016, while the top 10 alone have raised as much as the rest of the sector combined.

Off-grid PAYGo solar in Africa shows this clearly. Total investment fell by roughly a third between 2023 and 2024 and remained broadly flat in 2025, while the number of companies receiving any investment fell sharply, with first-time investments also declining. Roughly the same pool of capital chased a shrinking set of recipients, with names like Sun King, and d.light continuing to raise while smaller PAYGo companies shut down or were acquired. The concentration is even more pronounced among African-founded companies, whose share of funding fell sharply as larger, internationally backed operators continued to attract capital.

Battery swapping for African electric two-wheelers shows a similar pattern earlier in its cycle. Spiro has raised hundreds of millions of dollars since late 2025 and used it to push past 100,000 deployed motorcycles. MAX, backed by Equitane, Novastar, and Triple Jump, raised $24 million in the same period. Newer entrants received much smaller checks: ARC Ride took $5 million from the IFC, while Gogo Electric raised $1 million from ElectriFi.

What the two niches have in common helps explain the pattern. Within each, the underlying technology is increasingly mature and accessible. PAYGo solar companies use broadly similar panels, batteries and remote-lock hardware, supported by established mobile money collection rails. Battery-swapping companies rely on similar lithium battery systems, swap stations and imported electric motorcycles. Once the technology and business model are proven across a market, an investor is no longer underwriting whether the basic idea can work. The question becomes whether this specific team can execute it better than the next one, reach scale, and secure enough capital to keep expanding.

That creates an advantage for companies that are already operating at scale. An incumbent with years of collections data, established distribution and thousands of vehicles or solar systems deployed has evidence that a new entrant cannot manufacture in advance. It also has relationships with lenders and investors that can make the next round easier to raise. As these businesses mature, capital is also shifting toward larger debt and asset-backed facilities, where existing assets, proven collections and diversified cash flows matter. Capital therefore starts to reinforce operating scale: funding enables deployment, deployment creates customers and operating data, and that track record makes further funding easier to secure.

Framed this way, what separates winners from losers in a commoditized niche comes down to execution and access to funding. For new entrants, a third factor determines whether the first two are enough: whether the market is large enough, that well-funded early movers with strong execution cannot fully capture it. None of the three is about the technology.

For founders, this means building a verifiable execution record early, in whatever narrow segment the company can control tightly enough to prove it, while choosing investors deliberately. In a capital-intensive market, investors with the ability to provide follow-on capital and credibility with future lenders and investors can become part of the company’s competitive position. For funds writing early-stage checks where no execution record yet exists, diligence shifts accordingly: less toward whether the underlying model can work, since the sector has already demonstrated that it can, and more toward whether this founder can execute it, whether the market leaves room for another scaled player, and whether the company’s capital base can support the deployment needed to get there.
