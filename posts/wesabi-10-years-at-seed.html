WeSabi: 10 Years at Seed

Victor Ade-Oduntan · August 2026

Wesabi is a Lagos-based marketplace founded in 2015 by Murtala Sanni. The company set out to solve a specific problem in Nigeria’s informal artisan market: finding a reliable plumber, electrician, or carpenter without relying on word-of-mouth networks. Customers paid only after a job was completed, and the platform turned a hard, network-dependent task into a simple request, aiming to serve people with no existing artisan network of their own, such as renters and newcomers to a city, who previously had no reliable way to find help. Read broadly, the company was addressing a trust gap, competence of the artisan and safety of letting a stranger into a home.

In its early years, several signals pointed to real momentum. The founder brought business development and sales experience along with lived experience of the constraints of relying on informal networks to find reliable artisans. Wesabi had already won the Uncharted category at the African Entrepreneurship Awards in Morocco and signed a contract with Jaiz Bank in Abuja to serve as the bank’s artisan provider, both concrete signals of early market validation, with the enterprise relationship also pointing to B2B potential that could reduce reliance on consumer transactions. The broader environment supported that momentum too. Between 2011 and 2016, the share of Nigeria’s population using mobile phones rose from 68.5% to 81.0%, and internet users grew from 13.8% to 25.7%, creating conditions for platforms that would not have been viable a few years earlier. Home services marketplaces were also attracting serious capital abroad by this point, with Thumbtack alone raising USD267.5 million between 2013 and 2015 across three funding rounds, showing the model could scale into a large, durable business elsewhere, ahead of the space becoming crowded in Nigeria.

Ten years on, the company shows every sign of having quietly stalled. It has not raised a follow-on round since 2016. The app is not visible on either the Apple App Store or Google Play, and no independent press has covered the company since 2019.

The data suggests the core issue is structural rather than circumstantial. The pay-after-completion model made it easy for either side to move the relationship off the platform once an initial match was made, the customer could claim a job never started, or the artisan had little reason to complete it through the app at all. Once trust was established between a customer and an artisan, neither party had a strong reason to keep using the platform for future jobs. Market conditions reinforced this dynamic. Nigeria, Wesabi’s home market, is one where both customers and artisans tend to favour cutting out the middleman where possible, to reduce cost and simplify the process. That preference was sharpened by the economic conditions of the period, falling oil prices shrank the economy from 2014, and by the end of 2016 the National Bureau of Statistics reported that 17.0 million Nigerians were underemployed, a common sign of people seeking secondary income. Under that pressure, artisans had a tendency to treat Wesabi as one channel among several rather than a primary source of work, while customers had a stronger incentive to go direct and cut cost wherever they could.

Other players in the category addressed this same disintermediation risk directly. Urban Company, operating in India and the UAE, increased how often customers were prompted to return by expanding into beauty services and training artisans to build loyalty to the platform itself, something Wesabi’s single-service model never attempted. The underlying economics of the market compounded the problem. A Nigerian household likely needs a plumber or electrician once or twice a year, at a modest assumed cost of NGN10,000.0 to NGN20,000.0 per job. At an assumed platform take rate of 10% to 15%, that puts annual revenue per active household at roughly NGN1,000.0 to NGN6,000.0, a few dollars a year, economics that made it difficult to generate the kind of scale a business like this would need to grow substantially and attract further VC funding.
